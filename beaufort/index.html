Where it comes from

Francis Beaufort, an Irish-born officer of the Royal Navy, wrote his scale into his private journal around 1805–06, while commanding HMS Woolwich. He was not the first to grade the wind. Earlier scales existed, and he borrowed from them. What he added was a fixed set of numbers that every officer could log the same way.

The original scale did not mention speed at all. There were no anemometers on ships. Each force was defined by what a fully rigged man-of-war could carry: force 1 was a wind "just sufficient to give steerage way", force 12 was "that which no canvas could withstand". The sails were the instrument.

Robert FitzRoy used it on HMS Beagle, the ship that carried Darwin. In 1838 the Admiralty made it standard in all Royal Navy logs. When steam replaced sail, the sail descriptions stopped making sense. The descriptions of the sea surface that we use today, and the land descriptions (smoke, leaves, chimney pots), came in the late 19th and early 20th century; George Simpson of the Met Office set out land-based equivalents and speeds around 1906.

The speed equivalents follow a simple empirical law:

v ≈ 0.836 · B3/2 m/s

So force 4 is about 0.836 × 8 ≈ 6.7 m/s (13 kn), the middle of its 11–16 kn band, and force 12 lands near 35 m/s. Because of the 3/2 power, the steps get wider as you go up. Force 1 spans 2 knots; force 11 spans 7. Wind pressure on a sail grows roughly with the square of speed, so going from force 4 to force 8 means nearly three times the speed and roughly eight times the push on the rig.

Two caveats. The Met Office and WMO tables note that the wave heights are a guide for the open sea, far from land, and should not be used backwards to estimate the wind from the waves. And a Beaufort number describes a mean wind; gusts are stronger, sometimes by a force or two.
