The price of freedom

Most people measure money in things. I prefer to measure it in time. Every euro you keep is a small slice of a future where nobody has to pay you to show up. The question this page answers: how long is the crossing to the island where your investments cover your spending, and what sets the length of the trip?

Why the savings rate does almost all the work

Saving more pulls two levers at once. It fills the pile faster, and it shrinks the pile you need, because whatever you don't spend today is spending you have shown you can live without. At 10 % you save one euro for every nine you spend. At 50 % it is one for one. At 75 % you put away three euros for every euro you live on.

A surgeon who spends 95 % of her pay is further from the island than a gardener who saves half of his. Income matters through one door only: it makes a high savings rate easier to reach. target = yearly spending ÷ withdrawal rate

The model on this page is deliberately plain. Each year you invest a fixed share of a fixed income, the pile grows at a constant real return, and you arrive when the pile reaches the target. Real life is lumpier: incomes rise, kids happen, markets do not hand out the same return every year. The shape of the curve survives all of that. The exact number does not, so read it as "roughly".

This is not an argument against buying things. A wing that gets you on the water every windy afternoon is a fine trade for a few weeks of freedom. The point is to see the price in the currency you actually care about, and to notice that small monthly habits are the expensive ones: they are paid in perpetuity, and the target pile has to grow to fund them forever.

Real, not nominal

A bank statement shows nominal euros. Your future life is priced in real ones. If your investments return 7 % a year while prices rise 2 %, your purchasing power grows by about 4.9 %, not 7 %:

real return = (1 + nominal) ÷ (1 + inflation) − 1

Everything on this page is in real terms: returns above inflation, and targets in today's euros. That keeps the arithmetic honest without having to guess future price levels. For a sense of scale, Dimson, Marsh and Staunton's long-run data put world equities at roughly 5 % a year above inflation since 1900, with large differences between countries and decades. Bonds and cash have returned far less. A diversified portfolio sits somewhere in between, which is why 5 % is an optimistic but common default for a stock-heavy one, and why the band on the chart matters.

Where the "4 % rule" comes from

In 1994 William Bengen, a financial planner, asked a narrow question with US data going back to 1926: if a retiree withdraws a fixed share of the starting portfolio in year one, then the same amount adjusted for inflation every year after, what is the highest starting rate that never ran out of money within 30 years? For a portfolio of roughly half to three quarters US stocks, the answer was about 4 %. The worst starting year sat near the late 1960s, when inflation was high and markets went nowhere.

In 1998 three professors at Trinity University (Cooley, Hubbard and Walz) ran a similar test and reported success rates by withdrawal rate and horizon. Their tables became "the Trinity study", and together the two papers became "the 4 % rule".

The caveats are the interesting part

It was built for 30 years. Someone stopping work at 40 needs the pile to last 50 years or more. Longer horizons have lower historical safe rates, and analyses of long retirements often land near 3–3.5 %.

It is US data. The US had one of the best stock markets of the 20th century. Wade Pfau repeated Bengen's exercise for 17 developed countries in 2010 and found that 4 % would have failed in many of them; for most, the historical safe rate was lower.

The order of returns matters, not just their average. This is sequence-of-returns risk. While you are adding money, a crash is a sale. Once you are withdrawing, a crash early on forces you to sell cheap, and the pile may never recover. The two retirees below live through exactly the same 30 annual returns, in opposite order.

None of this makes 4 % wrong. It makes it a rule of thumb from one country's history, with a margin that shrinks as the horizon grows. Flexibility does a lot of the work in practice: spending a little less after a bad year, or earning a little on the island, changes the odds far more than a tenth of a point on the starting rate. Fees: the quiet headwind

A 1.6-point difference in yearly fees sounds like nothing. It compounds like everything else. Broad index funds and ETFs often charge 0.1–0.3 % a year. Actively managed funds sold through bank branches commonly charge 1.5–2 % or more, before any entry fees. Over a full crossing the gap is large.

A French footnote: the PEA

In France, the Plan d'Épargne en Actions is the usual wrapper for a long equity crossing. You can pay in up to €150,000. Once the plan is more than five years old, gains withdrawn are exempt from income tax. They are not exempt from social levies (prélèvements sociaux), which have been around 17–19 % depending on the year; check the rate in force when you withdraw. Withdrawing before five years generally closes the plan and the gains are taxed at the flat rate. Many low-fee ETFs tracking world indices are PEA-eligible through synthetic replication. The practical lesson: open one early, even with a small amount, because the five-year clock starts at opening, not at your first big deposit.

Not financial advice. This is a teaching toy with a constant-return model. It ignores taxes, pensions, inflation surprises, and the fact that your life will change. Markets can lose half their value and take a decade to recover. If you make real decisions, use your own numbers, read the sources, and consider an independent adviser who is not paid on commission.
