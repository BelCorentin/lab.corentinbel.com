1 · the leaking jug

Caffeine leaves by fractions, not by grams.

Your liver clears caffeine mostly through one enzyme, CYP1A2. At everyday doses the enzyme is far from busy, so the rate at which caffeine leaves is proportional to how much is there. Twice as much in the blood, twice as fast out. That is first-order elimination, and it has a fixed half-life: the time to lose half of whatever is present, whatever the amount.

amount(t) = dose × ½t / half-life

In healthy adults the half-life of caffeine averages about 5 hours, but it ranges from roughly 1.5 to 9.5 hours between people2. So a 16:00 coffee leaves about half its caffeine in you at 21:00 and a quarter at 02:00 for a typical person, and much more for a slow one.

Several things move the number a lot2,3:

factoreffect on caffeine half-life
smokingshorter, roughly by a third to a half. Chemicals in tobacco smoke (not nicotine itself) speed up CYP1A2. When people quit, caffeine lasts longer again.
oral contraceptivesroughly doubled.
pregnancylengthens progressively; in the third trimester it can reach around 15 hours.
genes (CYP1A2 variants), liver disease, some drugs (e.g. fluvoxamine)can make it much longer.

Because each dose decays on its own clock, the curve in the chart is simply a sum of exponentials, one per cup. Switch on each dose to see them stacked. Switch on log scale and every caffeine decay becomes a straight line with the same slope. That straight line is what "first order" looks like.

The European Food Safety Authority considers up to 400 mg a day (and single doses up to 200 mg) not a safety concern for healthy adults, and up to 200 mg a day during pregnancy. It also notes that around 100 mg close to bedtime can delay and shorten sleep in some adults1. In one controlled study, 400 mg taken even 6 hours before bed cut total sleep by more than an hour4.
2 · the queue at a single till

Alcohol leaves by grams per hour. That is the surprise.

Ethanol is cleared mainly by alcohol dehydrogenase in the liver. This enzyme is saturated at quite low blood levels, somewhere around 0.1 to 0.2 g/L. Above that, it works flat out and cannot go faster: the blood level falls by a roughly constant amount per hour, whatever you drank. That is zero-order elimination5.

In large forensic surveys the fall is about 0.10 to 0.25 g/L per hour, with 0.15 a common average for moderate drinkers (heavy drinkers sit at the upper end)5. For a 70 kg man that is roughly 7 grams of ethanol per hour, a bit less than one French standard drink (10 g) per hour. Coffee, cold showers and sleep do not change this rate.

So a half-life makes no sense for alcohol. Doubling the dose of caffeine adds one half-life to the time it takes to clear. Doubling the drinks doubles the time. Compare them here.

Blood alcohol, roughly: the Widmark formula

BAC (g/L) ≈ grams of ethanol ÷ (r × body weight in kg) − β × hours

Erik Widmark worked this out in the 1930s6. r is the share of the body that alcohol dilutes into, mostly water: about 0.68 on average for men and 0.55 for women, which is why the same drink gives women a higher level. β is the hourly fall. The bench adds a short absorption phase (slower with food) and lets elimination round off near zero, where the enzyme stops being saturated. Real values for one person can differ by 20% or more in either direction. Never use this to decide whether you can drive.

In France the legal limit is 0.5 g/L of blood (0.25 mg/L of exhaled air), and 0.2 g/L for probationary licence holders and bus and coach drivers7. A French standard drink holds about 10 g of ethanol: a 25 cl beer at 5%, a 10 cl glass of wine at 12%, or a 3 cl shot at 40%8.

3 · the long shadow

Nicotine is gone in hours. Cotinine is not.

A cigarette delivers on average about 1 mg of nicotine into the body, with wide variation depending on how it is smoked. Inhaled nicotine reaches the brain within seconds, and its half-life is about 2 hours. That short half-life is part of why smokers light up again through the day: the level falls back quickly between cigarettes9.

About 70 to 80% of nicotine is converted (mostly by CYP2A6) into cotinine, which has a half-life of about 16 hours9. It piles up across a day of smoking and is still there the next morning, which is why it is the standard biomarker of tobacco exposure. The dashed line in the nicotine lane shows it.

This is a teaching toy, not medical advice

The curves are textbook one-compartment models with population-average numbers. They ignore a lot: absorption from food and drink, tolerance, sleep, disease, medication, genetics, and the fact that "how much is in the blood" is not the same as "how impaired I am". Use it to build intuition, not to time a drive, a dose or a pregnancy decision. If you want to know which substances actually do the most harm, see the sibling page what's actually dangerous?.
