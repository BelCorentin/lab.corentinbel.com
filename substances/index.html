1 · one number per drug

When experts score sixteen kinds of harm, the top of the list is legal.

In 2010 David Nutt and colleagues of the Independent Scientific Committee on Drugs scored 20 drugs on 16 criteria: nine for harm to the user (deaths, damage to the body, dependence, loss of mental function, loss of relationships…) and seven for harm to other people (injury, crime, family breakdown, cost to the economy, international damage). The criteria were weighted and combined into one score out of 100 (multicriteria decision analysis, MCDA)1.

Alcohol came out on top, mainly because of what it does to other people. Heroin, crack and methamphetamine were the most harmful to the people who use them. The correlation between these scores and the drugs' legal class in the UK was 0.04, which the authors call "effectively no relation".

2 · body vs hook

Two separate questions: does it damage you, and does it hold on to you?

The earlier 2007 version of the panel scored each drug from 0 to 3 on physical harm, dependence and social harm2. Plotted against each other, the drugs spread out. Tobacco does only moderate physical harm on this scale (it kills slowly, over decades) but has one of the strongest pulls. LSD and ecstasy sit low on both.

x = mean physical harm, y = mean dependence, bubble area = social harm (all 0–3). Hover a bubble for the scores. Nutt et al. 2007, Lancet2; two independent expert groups agreed closely (r = 0.66–0.83 between groups, per the paper).

4 · how close is the edge

How many usual doses add up to a lethal one?

Robert Gable compiled, from published case reports and animal data, the ratio between a typical effective dose and a dose likely to kill: the safety ratio4. For injected heroin it is about 6. For alcohol it is about 10: drinking ten times your "nice evening" amount in one go can kill. Methamphetamine is also around 10, ketamine about 38. For LSD and psilocybin the estimate is about 1,000 (extrapolated, since well-documented fatal doses are lacking), and above 1,000 for cannabis.

Log scale. Lower = closer to the edge. Values are rough, for a single dose taken alone in a healthy adult; mixing depressants (alcohol + opioids + benzodiazepines + GHB) lowers the margin a lot. Gable 2004, Addiction; 2006, American Scientist4. A different method (margin of exposure, Lachenmeier & Rehm 20155) gives the same top four: alcohol, heroin, cocaine and nicotine in the high-risk range, cannabis the lowest.

5 · who gets hooked

Of 100 people who try it, how many become dependent?

The US National Comorbidity Survey (≈8,000 people) asked who had ever used each drug and who had ever met the criteria for dependence6. Tobacco "captures" about one in three people who try it, more than heroin.

Each grid = 100 people who ever used it; lit = met DSM-III-R dependence criteria at some point. Anthony, Warner & Kessler 19946.

A later and larger US survey (NESARC, ≈43,000 people) followed the whole lifetime and found higher numbers. Estimated lifetime probability of becoming dependent after first use7:

Lopez-Quintero et al. 2011, Drug and Alcohol Dependence7. Speed differs too: half of eventual cases appeared about 4 years after first use for cannabis and 5 for cocaine, but 13 for alcohol and 27 for nicotine.

6 · scale

Counting users and deaths.

Per dose, heroin is far more dangerous than a glass of wine. But billions of people drink and smoke, so the death toll is dominated by the two drugs you can buy at the corner shop.

7 · beyond the user's body

The harms that land on someone else.

Partner violence, sexual violence, crashes, children growing up around addiction, babies exposed in the womb, a mind that doesn't come back. These rarely appear in "harm per dose" numbers, and some are hard to measure at all. The criminologist Paul Goldstein sorted drug-related violence into three kinds, and only the first comes from the molecule itself22:

💥 pharmacological

the drug changes behaviour: disinhibition, aggression, paranoia. Alcohol is the textbook case; stimulants can do it too.

💸 economic-compulsive

stealing or robbing to pay for an expensive dependence. Mostly heroin, crack.

🔫 systemic

violence of the illegal market itself: turf wars, debts, punishing informers. It follows prohibition, not pharmacology (§8).

Editorial synthesis, not a measurement: each cell is our reading of the strength and size of the evidence in the sources cited on this page (hover a cell for its basis). A dim cell means little evidence of a large effect, or a question that is barely studied, not proof of safety.

🏠 behind closed doors

25 %

of perpetrators of the 138 intimate-partner killings in France in 2024 were under the influence of alcohol, 13 % of drugs23.

×1.4

odds of a man being violent to his partner when he drinks harmfully (meta-analysis of high-income countries, 2026)24. Problem use matters more than drug type: across 285 studies stimulants and other drugs gave similar links24.

1 in 8

US children live with a parent who has a substance use disorder; 1 in 10 with a parent with an alcohol use disorder25. Parental drug use is a factor in a third of US foster-care placements25.

⚠️ sexual violence and drugging

About half of sexual assaults involve alcohol drunk by the perpetrator, the victim or both26. This is about perpetrators' choices and opportunity, not victims' drinking. A separate harm is soumission chimique: deliberately drugging someone to assault them.

France, 2024: 1,377 suspected cases reported to the national survey (+30 % in a year). 85.5 % of victims are women, aged 9 months to 92 years; 96.4 % of perpetrators are men, and 57.9 % knew the victim. Sedative medicines (bromazepam, hydroxyzine, zopiclone, tramadol…) make up 59.6 % of substances mentioned; MDMA is the main illicit agent for the fourth year running; in probable cases involving adults, the drug was most often slipped into an alcoholic drink (44.8 %). ANSM / CEIP-A Paris27.

The Mazan trial made this visible: for nearly ten years Dominique Pelicot crushed lorazepam, a common anti-anxiety prescription, into his wife Gisèle's food and invited men to rape her unconscious. On 19 December 2024 he was sentenced to the maximum 20 years and all 50 co-defendants were convicted28. The drug was a prescription medicine.

🚗 on the road

Each square = 10 of France's 3,193 road deaths in 2024 (mainland). Alcohol over the 0.5 g/L limit was involved in 28.9 %, a positive drug test (mostly cannabis) in 19.6 %, either in 38.9 %; 53 % of drug-positive drivers were also over the alcohol limit. ONISR29. A driver positive for cannabis is ≈1.65× more likely to cause a fatal crash (ONISR); meta-analyses give ≈1.3×30; a drunk driver's risk is far higher. Worldwide, alcohol causes ≈27 % of road deaths31.

🧠 the mind

cannabis & psychosis

Daily use of high-potency cannabis: odds of a psychotic disorder ×4.8. If causal, ~12 % of new psychosis cases across 11 European sites, 30 % in London and 50 % in Amsterdam would be avoidable33. Causality is still debated.

methamphetamine

In pooled samples of people who use it (mostly from clinics and prisons), 36.5 % had had a methamphetamine-induced psychosis34.

psychedelics, low but not zero

Of 1,993 people describing their worst psilocybin trip, 11 % put themselves or others at risk, 2.6 % were violent and 7.6 % later sought help for lasting symptoms; 84 % still said they benefited35. In one survey 4.2 % reported distressing visual effects that persisted (HPPD)35.

alcohol, violence & suicide

Alcohol accounts for ≈18 % of deaths from interpersonal violence and up to 18 % of suicides worldwide31.

cocaine & the heart

Heart-attack risk rises ≈24-fold in the hour after taking cocaine (small study, 38 cases)36.

8 · the market

Prohibition doesn't remove the trade. It hands it to people who settle disputes with guns.

Illegal markets cannot use courts or contracts, so debts, territory and informers are handled with violence. That violence lands far from the user: on farmers in the Andes, dockworkers in Guayaquil and Antwerp, teenagers recruited in Marseille. Cocaine production is at a record: about 4,100 tonnes of pure cocaine in 2024, more than four times the 2014 low10.

9 · the law

Drug laws do not follow the harm ranking.

The UK classifies drugs as Class A (most harmful, harshest penalties), B or C. If the classes followed the evidence, the lines below would run roughly flat. They cross. The 2007 paper found no significant relationship between class and assessed harm (Kendall τ = −0.18, p = 0.25)2, and the 2010 scores correlate with class at 0.041; the Global Commission on Drug Policy described the international scheduling system as "when science was left behind"13.

Left: rank by total harm (Nutt 20101). Right: legal status in the UK under the Misuse of Drugs Act 1971, as of 2025. Ketamine moved from C to B in 2014, GHB from C to B in 2022, cannabis from C back to B in 2009.

The minister and the horse

In early 2009 Nutt, then chair of the government's Advisory Council on the Misuse of Drugs, published a short editorial comparing "equasy" (horse riding) with ecstasy: riding causes roughly one serious adverse event every 350 exposures, ecstasy about one in 10,00014. The Home Secretary asked him to apologise. In July 2009 he gave a lecture at King's College London ranking alcohol and tobacco above LSD, ecstasy and cannabis; on 30 October 2009 Home Secretary Alan Johnson dismissed him15. Council members resigned in protest, and Nutt set up the Independent Scientific Committee on Drugs, the group behind the 2010 ranking.

Who pays, who earns (France)

The economist Pierre Kopp estimated, for the French drug observatory, the yearly social cost of each drug: lives lost, lost quality of life, lost production, and public spending on care, police and prisons16.

The state does not even break even. Tobacco taxes (€13.1bn) are smaller than the public cost of treating smoking-related disease (€16.4bn); alcohol taxes (€4.0bn) cover about half of the cost of treating its harms (€7.8bn)16. And alcohol may still be advertised in France, under the restrictions of the 1991 Loi Évin, while tobacco advertising is banned.

10 · what these numbers don't say

Read this before quoting any chart above.

Expert scores are judgements

The MCDA ranking reflects one panel, its criteria and its weights. Critics argued that adding harm to users and harm to others into one number hides more than it shows, and that the scores reflect current laws and usage levels20. Alcohol's #1 is partly because so many people drink.

Prohibition changes the harm

Much of the harm of illegal drugs comes from unknown purity, adulteration (fentanyl in counterfeit pills), unsafe injection and the criminal market itself. A ranking measures the drug in its current legal setting, not the molecule.

Dose, route, frequency, context

One glass of wine a week and a bottle a day are different drugs in practice. Smoking cocaine (crack) and snorting it score very differently. Daily high-potency cannabis in adolescence carries risks (psychosis, dependence) that occasional adult use does not.

Mixtures kill

Most overdose deaths involve several substances. Safety ratios are for one drug alone; opioids + alcohol + benzodiazepines + GHB all slow breathing and add up.

Low harm ≠ no harm

Psychedelics score low for deaths and dependence, but can trigger panic, dangerous behaviour and, in susceptible people, lasting psychiatric problems. "Least harmful on the list" is not "safe".

Old numbers, changing drugs

Several sources are from the 1990s–2010s. Street potency has risen (cannabis THC, cocaine purity) and fentanyl has transformed the opioid market since. The direction of most findings has held up; the exact figures move.

If you use, or someone near you does

Don't mix depressants. Start low, wait, don't redose quickly. Test your drugs where drug checking exists. Carry naloxone if opioids are around (in France it is available without a prescription in pharmacies). Never use alone. In an emergency call 15 or 112 (France/EU) or 911 (US).
