    How fast is a nerve?

    Nerve signals are not electricity in a wire. They are waves of ion channels opening one after another, and their speed depends on the fibre. Thick fibres wrapped in myelin reach about 120 m/s. Thin bare ones crawl at 1 m/s or less.

    That is why stubbing a toe hurts twice: a sharp first pain carried by fast fibres, then a dull, burning second pain that arrives about a second later on slow ones.

    Pick a starting point and race the four main fibre types to the brain, in real time. The times count the nerve only; each relay adds a little.

    Why I use MEG

    Neurons talk with electrical currents. When tens of thousands of them push current the same way at the same moment, the tiny magnetic field they make can be picked up outside the head. That is magnetoencephalography: about 300 sensors in a helmet, sampling a thousand times a second, so it sees every wave on this page.

    fMRI measures something else: the blood that rushes in to refuel active tissue. That response is slow. It peaks about 5–6 seconds after the neural activity and takes 15 seconds or more to settle. The whole story above fits inside its first rising edge.

    The trade is space for time: fMRI localises to millimetres, MEG is blurrier in space. For decoding words, which arrive every few hundred milliseconds, time wins. In my research I train decoders on these signals to read out which word someone heard, or only imagined.

    Sources and caveats

    Latencies are approximate and vary with the stimulus, the person, loudness or contrast, and how a wave is measured. Wave names (N1, N400…) come from EEG; MEG has close counterparts (M100, N400m). Brain regions are where these responses are mostly thought to come from, drawn on a cartoon; real sources are spread out and overlap in time.
