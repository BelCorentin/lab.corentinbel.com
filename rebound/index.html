we made it efficient.
so why do we use more?

The rebound effect is one of the least intuitive ideas in climate economics, and one of the most important. This page starts with a question about AI. Don't look up the answer: make a guess, and we'll come back to it at the very end.

your guess

The price of running an AI model at a given level of ability is falling by roughly ten times a year.16 Each answer needs less and less electricity. So, five years from now, the total electricity used by AI will be…

1 · the idea

cheaper per use, so more uses

You swap your car for one that burns half as much fuel. An engineer would say: same trips, half the fuel. But each kilometre now costs you half as much, so you take the car a bit more often: the trip to the beach, the friend who lives far away. Part of the fuel you saved gets burned again. That part is the rebound.

rebound = 1 − (savings you actually get ÷ savings the engineer promised)

Play with it. The old car burns 8 L per 100 km over 10,000 km a year.

try

"Typical": studies of drivers usually find that 10 to 30 % of the promised fuel saving is eaten back.4

Three zones, worth remembering:

  • 0 %: the savings arrive in full. Rare outside a spreadsheet.
  • between 0 and 100 %: the savings shrink but stay real. This is by far the most common case.
  • above 100 %: backfire. The efficiency gain ends up increasing total consumption. This is the Jevons paradox.
2 · five surprising questions

how it has played out, for real

Guess first, then open the card.

11865. Watt's steam engine burns far less coal per unit of work than the old Newcomen pumps. So why is Britain burning more coal than ever?open

That's the question William Stanley Jevons asked in The Coal Question. His answer became the paradox named after him:

“It is wholly a confusion of ideas to suppose that the economical use of fuel is equivalent to a diminished consumption. The very contrary is the truth.”1

Efficient engines made steam power cheap enough to be worth using everywhere: mines, mills, railways, ships. Each engine used less coal; there were vastly more engines. Jevons himself noted that engine efficiency had risen at least tenfold in under a century, and British coal output kept climbing: about 65 million tons in 1854, 98 million in 1865, and 287 million by 1913.1,2 The idea came back in the 1980s, when economists Daniel Khazzoom and Leonard Brookes argued that efficiency standards would save less than promised; it is now called the Khazzoom–Brookes postulate.11

2Light is thousands of times cheaper than in 1800. Do we spend less on lighting?open

In the UK between 1800 and 2000, the price of a unit of light fell to less than 1/3000 of its 1800 value. Over the same period, each person came to use about 6,500 times more light, while incomes rose only about 15 times.3 So the share of income spent on light did fall, but the amount of energy turned into light exploded.

Candles, then gas, then kerosene, then incandescent bulbs, then LEDs: every jump in efficiency was met by more light, in more rooms, for more hours, and in places that used to be dark (streets, shop windows, screens). We didn't light the same house for less. We lit the world.

3Car engines are much more efficient than in 1980. Why hasn't a new car's fuel use fallen anywhere near as much?open

Because the gains went into weight and power. The economist Christopher Knittel estimated that if American cars had kept the weight, horsepower and torque of 1980, their fuel economy could have risen by nearly 60 % between 1980 and 2006. Most of the engineering progress was spent on heavier, faster cars instead.7 Globally, SUVs were the second-largest contributor to the growth of global CO₂ emissions in the 2010s, after the power sector and ahead of heavy industry, trucks and aviation.8

This is a quieter kind of rebound: the gain is not spent on more driving, but on bigger driving. Same fuel, more car.

4You insulate your house. Will your bill fall by what the energy audit promised?open

Usually not. For heating in rich countries, the best estimates put the direct rebound at 10 to 30 % of the promised saving.4,5 And there is a twist: in a study of 3,400 German homes, measured heating use was on average about 30 % below what the energy rating predicted, and around 40 % below for the worst-rated houses.6 The audit's promised saving was calculated from a consumption that never existed.

Two things happen. After the works, people heat to a more comfortable temperature (a rebound: the cold house was being under-heated). And before the works, poorly-rated houses were already using much less than their rating said, because people were heating them less (a prebound). Both shrink the real saving. Neither makes insulation useless: people are warmer, and the bill does fall.

5Trains, cars and planes are far faster than walking. Do we spend less time travelling?open

No. Across very different societies and centuries, people spend on average about one hour a day travelling. The physicist Cesare Marchetti called it an "anthropological invariant", building on the transport researcher Yacov Zahavi.9 When people got faster, they did not travel less: they travelled farther, in the same hour.

The rebound here is in distance, not energy: speed was never turned into free time, it was turned into living farther from work, shopping farther, and holidays farther away. The same logic applies to almost any efficiency, including your own time: a tool that saves you an hour usually gets you more work, not more holiday.

3 · how far the ripples go

three kinds of rebound

Drop an efficiency gain in the pond. The ripples go further than your own car.

direct

you use more of the same thing

You drive the efficient car more, heat the insulated house warmer, leave the LED on. The easiest to measure.

indirect

you spend the savings elsewhere

The €300 you no longer spend on fuel buys a weekend flight, a new phone, a bigger TV. All of that needed energy to make.

economy-wide

everything gets cheaper to make

Firms using cheaper energy lower their prices, sell more, grow, invent new uses. This is the scale Jevons was talking about, and the hardest to measure.

so how big is it, really?

whattypical sizeevidence
car travel, direct10–30 %17 studies, rich countries4
home heating, direct10–30 %9 studies, rich countries4,5
whole economyoften > 50 %"may erode more than half of the expected energy savings"10
backfire (> 100 %)rare"little support" in measured household and firm cases14; plausible for general-purpose technologies4

Honest summary. At the level of your own car or house, rebound usually eats a minority of the gain: efficiency still saves energy. At the level of a whole economy, the size is genuinely disputed, and the most recent reviews say it is bigger than most energy models assume. Backfire is real but it tends to show up for general-purpose technologies that open new uses: steam, light, computing. Keep that in mind for the end.

4 · climate and energy policy

why efficiency alone won't get us there

Almost every climate plan counts heavily on efficiency: better buildings, better engines, better appliances. The rebound effect doesn't say this is wrong. It says efficiency is a gain, and a gain gets spent unless something decides what it's spent on.

The IPCC now says it plainly: well-designed policies have helped address "rebound effects", and its last report adds a third pillar next to efficiency and clean energy, sufficiency.12 France's 2026 low-carbon strategy (SNBC-3) explicitly asks to "maîtriser l'effet rebond post-rénovation" (keep post-renovation rebound under control) and to stick to 19 °C heating.22 Four levers stop the gain from being spent again:

lever 1

put a ceiling on the total

If total emissions are capped, a rebound in one place has to be offset somewhere else under the same cap. That is the logic of the EU Emissions Trading System for power and industry: the cap, not the efficiency, sets the total.23 Caveat: since its 2018 reform the cap partly adjusts to demand (the "punctured waterbed"), and it doesn't cover everything.

lever 2

keep the price of energy where it was

Rebound happens because efficiency makes each use cheaper. A carbon or energy tax that rises as efficiency improves can keep the cost per use stable, so the gain shows up as less energy, not as more use. Returning the revenue to households (a "carbon dividend") keeps it fair, though spending that money creates a bit of indirect rebound too.

lever 3

sufficiency (sobriété)

Deciding what is enough: a smaller car, a 19 °C living room, a train instead of a short flight, a smaller model for a simple task. The IPCC defines sufficiency as measures and daily practices that "avoid demand for energy, materials, land and water while delivering human well-being for all".12 In France, the négaWatt association builds its scenarios on the same three pillars: sufficiency, efficiency, renewables, in that order.13

lever 4

count the rebound when you plan

Many energy models used to set targets assume little or no rebound. Building it in (or at least checking the plan still works with 20 to 50 % rebound) avoids promising savings that won't arrive, and shifts money toward measures that actually lower totals.

the two wrong conclusions

  • "Rebound means efficiency is useless." No. In most measured cases most of the saving stays, and even the part that rebounds buys something real (a warmer home, more mobility, more light). Without efficiency, every other lever is more expensive.14
  • "Efficiency will fix it on its own." No either. Without a cap, a price or a choice to stop at "enough", part of every gain is spent again, and for transformative technologies possibly all of it.
5 · back to the question

computing: the rebound happening right now

If there is one technology that fits Jevons' description, it is the computer. From 1946 to 2009, the number of computations you could do with one kilowatt-hour doubled every 1.57 years: forty doublings in sixty years.15 Nobody used that to run the 1960 workload on a watch battery. We put computers in every pocket, car and fridge.

data centres: the brake, then the boom

2010 and 2018: Masanet et al.; 2024 and 2030: IEA. Different methods, so read the shape, not the exact gap.

AI: price falls ×1000, use rises ×100

Left: price per million tokens for GPT-3-level ability (a16z). Right: tokens processed per month across Google products (Google).

For a while, efficiency won. Between 2010 and 2018, the work done in data centres grew about 550 % while their electricity use grew only 6 %, thanks to better chips and huge, efficient cloud facilities.20 Then AI arrived. Data centres used about 415 TWh in 2024 (around 1.5 % of world electricity), their demand jumped 17 % in 2025 alone, and the IEA expects it to roughly double by 2030, with AI-focused use tripling.18,19

Meanwhile, the price of a given level of AI ability fell about tenfold a year: GPT-3-level answers cost $60 per million tokens in 2021 and $0.06 three years later.16,17 And use exploded: Google went from processing about 9.7 trillion tokens a month in 2024 to 480 trillion a year later, then over 1.3 quadrillion by late 2025.21 A thousandfold drop in price, a hundredfold rise in use within a year and a half: textbook Jevons.

The answer is much higher. The IEA's projections point up, not down. Not because efficiency fails: each answer really does need less energy. But AI is a general-purpose technology, like steam, light and computing before it, and for those, cheaper has always meant much more.

what it means for code, compute and AI

  • A cheaper token is not a smaller footprint. The footprint is cost per token × number of tokens, and the second number is growing faster than the first is shrinking. (The sibling page token footprint computes the first number; this page is about the second.)
  • Agents multiply calls. A coding assistant that reads a whole repository, plans, runs tests and retries makes hundreds of model calls where a chatbot made one. Every efficiency gain makes it affordable to let them do more.
  • The lever is the same as for energy. A grid that is decarbonised (a cap on emissions, not on compute), prices that include the cost of power, and choosing when "enough model" is enough for the task.
